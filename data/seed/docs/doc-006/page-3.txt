Article Xill. Association Dues and Other Charges
        Where the property is subject to a homeowners or condominium association, the assessments levied by that association are your direct responsibility and are
        ordinarily not escrowed. Unpaid assessments can become a lien on the property under NY law, and in some jurisdictions a portion of that lien has priority over
        the mortgage. Utility charges, in certain jurisdictions, may likewise become a lien if unpaid.
        Keep association assessments and utility obligations current, and retain evidence of payment. If an association changes its billing address or management
       company, update your records promptly so that invoices continue to reach you.
        Article XIV. If Taxes or Premiums Go Unpaid
        Where an obligation on the property goes unpaid, the security instrument permits the lender to advance the funds necessary to protect its interest, including
        paying delinquent taxes or purchasing insurance coverage, and to recover the advance from you together with interest at the rate provided in the loan
        documents. An advance of that kind is added to the amount you owe.
        An advance is a remedy of last resort rather than a service, and it is materially more expensive than paying the obligation yourself. Where an escrow account
        exists, advances are rare because the servicer pays the escrowed items directly; where no escrow account exists, the responsibility to monitor and pay each
         bill rests entirely with you.

         Article XVII. If the Property Is Damaged
         Report damage to your insurance carrier promptly, document the condition with photographs before repairs begin, and retain every estimate, invoice, and
         receipt. Notify the servicer as well, because the security instrument gives the lender an interest in the proceeds and, for a significant loss, a loss-draft process
         governs how funds are released.
          Continue making your monthly payments while a claim is pending. A casualty does not suspend the obligation to pay, and the two matters are handled on
          separate tracks; allowing the loan to fall behind while a claim is processed creates a second problem without resolving the first.
